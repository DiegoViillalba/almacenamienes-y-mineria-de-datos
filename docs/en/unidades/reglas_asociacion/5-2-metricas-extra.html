1 Introduction

The previous chapter established the foundations of association rule analysis: definitions of support, confidence, and lift, the rule generation process via the Apriori algorithm, and basic criteria for distinguishing genuine rules from trivial ones. The central argument was that generating rules is algorithmically simple, but critical evaluation of their validity demands moving beyond confidence as the sole criterion.

This chapter continues that line of reasoning and explores four directions that introductory treatments leave open. First, we rigorously analyze the structural limitations of lift as an evaluation metric: although lift corrects the bias of confidence, it possesses its own deficiencies that specialized literature has documented in detail, which are often ignored in introductory presentations. Second, we introduce a set of complementary metrics —leverage, conviction, and Zhang’s metric— whose mathematical properties allow evaluating aspects of rules that lift fails to capture, and whose combination yields more robust evaluations. Third, we address the issue of parameter sensitivity: we empirically demonstrate that the number, nature, and quality of rules obtained depend non-trivially on thresholds set by the analyst when invoking the algorithm, carrying direct methodological consequences. Finally, we propose representing the rule set as a relationship graph between items, enabling identification of the global structure of rule space and distinguishing core patterns from peripheral ones.

The common thread running through all these sections is identical to the one that motivated the previous chapter: the objective is not to maximize the number of reported rules, but to identify the small subset of rules representing nontrivial patterns, backed by consistent evidence from multiple perspectives.

2 Structural Limitations of Lift

2.1 The Symmetry of Lift and the Asymmetry of Decisions

An algebraic property of lift that few introductory presentations discuss in depth is its symmetry: for any pair of itemsets \(A\) and \(B\),

\[\text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(A) \cdot P(B)} = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(B) \cdot P(A)} = \text{lift}(B \Rightarrow A) \tag{1}\]

Symmetry follows directly from the commutativity of multiplication in the denominator. Lift measures the deviation of joint probability \(P(A \cup B)\) from expected value under independence \(P(A) \cdot P(B)\), and this deviation is identical regardless of which set is designated as antecedent or consequent.

This symmetry has a major practical consequence that frequently goes unnoticed. When the algorithm generates the two rules \(A \Rightarrow B\) and \(B \Rightarrow A\) from the same frequent itemset \(\{A, B\}\), both rules will have the exact same lift. However, their confidences will, in general, differ:

\[\text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(A)}, \qquad \text{conf}(B \Rightarrow A) = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(B)}\]

The rule with higher confidence is the one whose antecedent has lower individual support. If \(P(A) < P(B)\), then \(\text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B) > \text{conf}(B \Rightarrow A)\): predicting the more frequent item from the less frequent item is “easier” in terms of confidence, precisely because the consequent occurs with higher baseline frequency.

Confidence, despite its documented limitations, remains the relevant metric for directing that choice. A recommendation engine sorting rules purely by lift and picking the first direction encountered may be recommending the suboptimal direction.

2.2 Theoretical Ceiling of Lift and Cross-Rule Comparability

A second limitation of lift, equally critical and less widely known, is that its scale is not universal: the maximum achievable lift for a given pair depends on individual item frequencies. This property makes comparing lifts across rules with different individual support levels methodologically flawed in a strict sense.

To derive the theoretical ceiling, observe that co-occurrence support \(P(A \cup B)\) is bounded above by the support of the rarer item: if \(P(A) \leq P(B)\), then \(P(A \cup B) \leq P(A)\) (no more transactions can co-occur than those containing the rarer item). Therefore:

\[\text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(A) \cdot P(B)} \leq \frac{P(A)}{P(A) \cdot P(B)} = \frac{1}{P(B)} = \frac{1}{\max(P(A), P(B))} \tag{2}\]

The maximum theoretical lift for the pair \((A, B)\) is:

\[\text{lift}_{\max}(A, B) = \frac{1}{\max(P(A), P(B))} \tag{3}\]

This result has an immediate consequence: two frequent items —say, with support 0.50 each— have a maximum theoretical lift of just 2.0. Two rare items —with support 0.05 each— have a maximum theoretical lift of 20. An observed lift of 1.8 for the first pair represents 90% of its achievable maximum; the same lift of 1.8 for the second pair represents only 9%. The exact same figure signals a near-optimal rule in one case and a weak rule in the other.

To correct for this scale asymmetry, one can compute relative lift, defined as the fraction of theoretical maximum achieved by the observed lift:

\[\text{lift}_{\text{rel}}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{\text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B)}{\text{lift}_{\max}(A, B)} = \text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) \cdot \max(P(A), P(B)) \tag{4}\]

Relative lift spans the range \((0, 1]\) and is directly comparable across rules with different frequency levels. A relative lift near 1 indicates that items co-occur with maximum possible frequency given their individual supports.

3 Complementary Metrics to Lift

The catalog of proposed metrics for evaluating association rule interest is extensive: Tan et al. (2006) systematically enumerate and compare over twenty distinct measures. None is universally superior; each captures a distinct facet of what we intuitively call “interest.” This section details three metrics whose mathematical properties and complementarity with lift make them especially valuable in practice.

3.1 Leverage: Absolute Difference Above Chance

Leverage for a rule \(A \Rightarrow B\) measures the absolute difference between observed co-occurrence frequency and expected frequency under statistical independence:

\[\text{lev}(A \Rightarrow B) = P(A \cup B) - P(A) \cdot P(B) \tag{5}\]

This definition shares the exact structure of lift’s numerator, minus the normalization by \(P(A) \cdot P(B)\). Consequently, leverage preserves probability units, giving it an operationally direct interpretation: a leverage of 0.05 in a 1,000-transaction dataset means roughly 50 transactions contain both \(A\) and \(B\) beyond expectation under independence. This is the additional transaction count attributable to the association.

The theoretical range of leverage is \([-\min(P(A), P(B)) \cdot (1 - \max(P(A), P(B))),\; \min(P(A), P(B)) \cdot (1 - \max(P(A), P(B)))]\), centered at zero. Positive leverage indicates positive association; negative indicates inhibition; zero indicates independence —just like lift, but on an absolute rather than relative scale.

The key distinction between leverage and lift lies in scale. Lift normalizes deviation from independence into a dimensionless number comparable across rules with different consequents. Leverage gives the absolute magnitude of that deviation, which is more interpretable when quantifying rule impact: in recommendation engines, leverage is proportional to additional transactions generated by acting on the rule. However, this reliance on absolute frequencies makes leverage systematically low for rare items, even when their relative relationship is very strong —the exact inverse problem of absolute lift.

3.2 Conviction: Asymmetry and Quasi-Determinism

Conviction for a rule \(A \Rightarrow B\) was introduced to create a directional, asymmetric metric like confidence that simultaneously reflects departure from statistical independence (Tan et al. 2006):

\[\text{conv}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{1 - P(B)}{1 - \text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B)} = \frac{P(A) \cdot (1 - P(B))}{P(A) - P(A \cup B)} \tag{6}\]

Conviction’s interpretation is less intuitive than lift or leverage, but warrants careful examination. The numerator \(1 - P(B)\) is the expected frequency of rule “errors” —transactions where \(B\) does not occur— under independence: if \(A\) and \(B\) were independent, a fraction \(1 - P(B)\) of transactions containing \(A\) would lack \(B\). The denominator \(1 - \text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B)\) is the observed frequency of those errors. The ratio measures how many times fewer errors the rule makes compared to expectation under independence.

Several notable properties follow:

  • When \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, \(\text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B) = P(B)\), and conviction \(= (1 - P(B)) / (1 - P(B)) = 1\). Conviction equals 1 if and only if there is independence —just as lift equals 1 under independence.
  • As confidence approaches 1, the denominator approaches 0, and conviction approaches infinity. A rule with very high conviction is quasi-deterministic: observing \(A\) predicts \(B\) with near certainty.
  • When confidence is lower than \(P(B)\) (negative association), conviction is less than 1.
  • Unlike lift, conviction is asymmetric: \(\text{conv}(A \Rightarrow B) \neq \text{conv}(B \Rightarrow A)\) in general, making it well-suited for evaluating rule strength in its specific direction.

This asymmetry is particularly valuable when rule direction carries operational meaning: in recommendation systems, medical diagnostics, or any domain where acting on an antecedent predicts a consequent.

3.3 Zhang’s Metric: Bounded Range and Robustness

Proposed to address limitations of lift, Zhang’s metric is defined as (Tan et al. 2006):

\[Z(A, B) = \frac{P(A \cup B) - P(A) \cdot P(B)}{\max\left\{P(A \cup B)\cdot[1 - P(A)],\; P(A)\cdot[P(B) - P(A \cup B)]\right\}} \tag{7}\]

The numerator matches leverage: observed co-occurrence minus independence. The denominator normalizes the metric to ensure \(Z \in [-1, 1]\) for any item pair, regardless of individual frequencies. This property —lacking in lift— makes Zhang’s metric directly comparable across rules with varying frequency levels.

The denominator selects the maximum of two terms: \(P(A \cup B) \cdot [1 - P(A)]\), which dominates under high co-occurrence (strong positive association), and \(P(A) \cdot [P(B) - P(A \cup B)]\), which dominates under low co-occurrence (negative association or inhibition). This design guarantees a symmetric scale: \(Z = 1\) corresponds to maximum co-occurrence (\(A\) and \(B\) co-occur whenever the rarer item occurs), while \(Z = -1\) corresponds to minimum co-occurrence (mutual exclusion).

Zhang’s metric is symmetric —like lift— and exceptionally robust against highly frequent items, which cause the most severe bias in confidence and leverage. For these reasons, Tan et al. (2006) recommend Zhang’s metric as one of the most reliable measures for ranking rules by general interest when no prior domain knowledge is available.

Figure 37.2 reveals several structural relationships. First, lift and Zhang show high positive correlation: both measure essential deviation from independence, differing in scale and extreme-value behavior. Second, leverage and support show moderate positive correlation: frequent items produce higher absolute leverage, reflecting leverage’s dependence on base frequencies. Third, conviction can exhibit low correlation with other metrics in certain datasets, precisely because it is sensitive to confidence values near 1 —where minor confidence shifts produce large conviction changes— while other metrics change smoothly.

Regions of low or negative correlation in this matrix are analytically most informative: where two metrics disagree, the analyst must decide which to weight higher based on context. A rule with high lift but low leverage involves infrequent items whose relative association is strong but whose absolute impact is limited. A rule with high Zhang but moderate conviction may be robust in overall association while not quasi-deterministic in direction.

4 Sensitivity to Algorithm Parameters

4.1 Thresholds as Decisions Lacking Intrinsic Statistical Basis

When invoking Apriori with specific parameters —e.g., min_support = 0.10 and minimum lift of 1.0— decisions are made that, unlike parameters in formal statistical models, carry no intrinsic statistical justification. No theory establishes that the “correct” minimum support is 0.10 rather than 0.08 or 0.12. Analysts select these thresholds based on practical considerations —compute time, manageable result set size, domain knowledge— but the algorithm accepts them blindly.

This circumstance carries serious methodological consequences: the resulting rule set is not an objective property of the dataset, but a joint function of the dataset and chosen parameters. Two analysts working on the same dataset with slightly different thresholds can arrive at substantially different rule sets, potentially reaching conflicting conclusions if unaware of this dependence. Sound methodology requires exploring result stability across reasonable parameter shifts —termed sensitivity analysis— and reporting those parameters alongside findings.

The behavior in Figure 37.4 is neither accidental nor dataset-specific: it is a structural property of rule mining. Raising \(\sigma_{\min}\) excludes less frequent itemsets. Many of these involve rare items co-occurring by chance in the sample —precisely the patterns most vulnerable to statistical instability. Filtering them leaves a remaining rule set with higher average support and higher median lift. Informally: patterns surviving stricter filters are, on average, more robust.

The tension between these curves reflects the fundamental trade-off in association rule mining: one cannot simultaneously maximize rule quantity and average quality. Every support threshold represents a choice within this trade-off space, governed by the relative cost of two error types: including spurious rules (false positives) versus omitting genuine but rare patterns (false negatives).

High-density regions (bottom-left, deep red) represent loose thresholds yielding high rule counts —along with high numbers of spurious rules. The critical methodological lesson is: no single point on this map is correct in the abstract. Threshold choices are always relative to problem context. In retail with millions of daily transactions, min_support = 0.01 is justified because 1% represents thousands of observations. In a medical dataset with 200 patients, that same threshold equals just 2 cases —statistically inadequate for any claim. Correct selection requires translating support percentages into absolute observation counts and judging whether that sample size supports the intended claim.

5 Global Structure of Rule Space: Graph Representation

5.1 Motivation for a Network Representation

Previous sections represented rules as tables and metric scatter plots. Both forms help evaluate individual rules or small subsets, but obscure the relational structure connecting items globally. An item can participate in many rules as antecedent or consequent; two items may connect directly via a rule or indirectly through an intermediate item. This topological structure is invisible in tabular form.

Graph representations make this structure explicit. Formally, we construct a graph \(G = (V, E)\) where vertex set \(V\) comprises unique items participating in at least one filtered rule, and edge set \(E\) contains directed edge \((A, B)\) for every rule \(A \Rightarrow B\) exceeding minimum lift and support thresholds. To maintain readability, we restrict focus to single-item antecedents and consequents (1-itemsets).

5.2 Centrality, Hubs, and Bridge Nodes

In graph theory, node centrality measures relative importance in the network. Among multiple definitions, a natural metric in association rules is the sum of lifts for all rules involving the item as antecedent or consequent. This weights items participating in many strong associations, rather than merely many associations.

6 An Integrated Evaluation Framework

6.1 From Fragmentation to Coherence

The concepts developed across this chapter —lift limitations, complementary metrics, parameter sensitivity, graph structure— are not isolated components. They form a unified framework answering a core question: how much confidence does a specific rule warrant?

Answering this question involves multiple dimensions:

  1. Relative Dimension: How far does observed lift deviate from independence relative to theoretical maximum given item supports? Relative lift addresses this.

  2. Absolute Dimension: How many additional transactions beyond baseline independence exhibit co-occurrence? Leverage answers this.

  3. Directional Predictive Dimension: How deterministic is the rule in its specific direction? Conviction addresses this.

  4. Normalized Robustness Dimension: Is the association strong on a scale comparable across varying item frequencies? Zhang’s metric resolves this.

  5. Parametric Stability Dimension: Does the rule persist across reasonable threshold choices, or vanish under slight \(\sigma_{\min}\) tightening? Sensitivity analysis addresses this.

A rule showing strength across all dimensions earns high confidence. A rule succeeding in only one or two calls for caution. The following function systematically executes this multidimensional evaluation:

    Multidimensional evaluation of an association rule.
    Returns qualitative classification: ROBUST / CANDIDATE / DISCARD
    """
    criterios = []
    puntos = 0

    if row["lift"] > 1.5:

        puntos += 3
    elif row["lift"] > 1.1:
        criterios.append("Moderate lift (1.1 – 1.5)")
        puntos += 1

        puntos += 2

        puntos += 1
    else:
        criterios.append("Weak Zhang (≤ 0.1) — likely base frequency artifact")

7 Conceptual Summary

This chapter explored association rule mining beyond fundamental metrics. Key takeaways:

On Structural Limitations of Lift:

  • Lift is symmetric: \(\text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) = \text{lift}(B \Rightarrow A)\). Selecting rule direction for practical applications relies on confidence —an asymmetric metric.
  • Lift has a theoretical ceiling dependent on individual supports: \(\text{lift}_{\max} = 1 / \max(P(A), P(B))\). Comparing raw lifts across rules with differing supports can be misleading. Relative lift —ratio of observed to maximum lift— provides a fairer basis for comparison.

On Complementary Metrics:

  • Leverage measures absolute difference between observed co-occurrence and independence. It reflects additional transactions attributable to association, but is systematically low for rare items.
  • Conviction is asymmetric and measures rule quasi-determinism in direction: very high values signal confidence near 1.
  • Zhang’s metric spans \([-1, 1]\), is symmetric, and resists frequent-item bias. It is recommended for comparing rules across varying frequency regimes.
  • Metrics are non-redundant: imperfect correlations mean metric discrepancies offer key analytical signals.

On Parameter Sensitivity:

  • Apriori thresholds carry no intrinsic statistical justification. Resulting rule sets are a joint function of dataset and chosen parameters.
  • Sensitivity analysis —systematic exploration of parameter space— is a mandatory methodological practice.
  • The support-confidence map visualizes full decision space, letting analysts deliberately position choices along the quantity vs. quality curve.

On Graph Representations:

  • Rule graphs reveal network topology: hubs, bridge nodes, and item clusters.
  • Lift-weighted centrality —rather than raw edge counts— distinguishes genuinely central items from frequency-driven artifacts.
  • The “Whole Milk Problem” demonstrates how high consequent frequency makes discovering informative rules nearly impossible regardless of thresholds.

On the Integrated Framework:

  • Robust rule evaluation requires examining at least four dimensions simultaneously: relative lift, leverage, Zhang, and conviction.
  • Robust rules show convergence across multiple metrics; candidate rules show partial convergence; discarded rules fail under stricter metrics.

## Introduction {#sec-intro-cap2}

The previous chapter established the foundations of association rule analysis: definitions of support, confidence, and lift, the rule generation process via the Apriori algorithm, and basic criteria for distinguishing genuine rules from trivial ones. The central argument was that generating rules is algorithmically simple, but critical evaluation of their validity demands moving beyond confidence as the sole criterion.

This chapter continues that line of reasoning and explores four directions that introductory treatments leave open. First, we rigorously analyze the *structural limitations of lift* as an evaluation metric: although lift corrects the bias of confidence, it possesses its own deficiencies that specialized literature has documented in detail, which are often ignored in introductory presentations. Second, we introduce a set of *complementary metrics* —leverage, conviction, and Zhang's metric— whose mathematical properties allow evaluating aspects of rules that lift fails to capture, and whose combination yields more robust evaluations. Third, we address the issue of *parameter sensitivity*: we empirically demonstrate that the number, nature, and quality of rules obtained depend non-trivially on thresholds set by the analyst when invoking the algorithm, carrying direct methodological consequences. Finally, we propose representing the rule set as a *relationship graph* between items, enabling identification of the global structure of rule space and distinguishing core patterns from peripheral ones.

The common thread running through all these sections is identical to the one that motivated the previous chapter: the objective is not to maximize the number of reported rules, but to identify the small subset of rules representing nontrivial patterns, backed by consistent evidence from multiple perspectives.

## Structural Limitations of Lift {#sec-limites-lift}

### The Symmetry of Lift and the Asymmetry of Decisions

An algebraic property of lift that few introductory presentations discuss in depth is its *symmetry*: for any pair of itemsets $A$ and $B$,

$$\text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(A) \cdot P(B)} = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(B) \cdot P(A)} = \text{lift}(B \Rightarrow A)$$ {#eq-lift-simetria}

Symmetry follows directly from the commutativity of multiplication in the denominator. Lift measures the deviation of joint probability $P(A \cup B)$ from expected value under independence $P(A) \cdot P(B)$, and this deviation is identical regardless of which set is designated as antecedent or consequent.

This symmetry has a major practical consequence that frequently goes unnoticed. When the algorithm generates the two rules $A \Rightarrow B$ and $B \Rightarrow A$ from the same frequent itemset $\{A, B\}$, both rules will have the exact same lift. However, their confidences will, in general, differ:

$$\text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(A)}, \qquad \text{conf}(B \Rightarrow A) = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(B)}$$

The rule with higher confidence is the one whose antecedent has *lower* individual support. If $P(A) < P(B)$, then $\text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B) > \text{conf}(B \Rightarrow A)$: predicting the more frequent item from the less frequent item is "easier" in terms of confidence, precisely because the consequent occurs with higher baseline frequency.

### Theoretical Ceiling of Lift and Cross-Rule Comparability

A second limitation of lift, equally critical and less widely known, is that its scale is not universal: the maximum achievable lift for a given pair depends on individual item frequencies. This property makes comparing lifts across rules with different individual support levels methodologically flawed in a strict sense.

To derive the theoretical ceiling, observe that co-occurrence support $P(A \cup B)$ is bounded above by the support of the rarer item: if $P(A) \leq P(B)$, then $P(A \cup B) \leq P(A)$ (no more transactions can co-occur than those containing the rarer item). Therefore:

$$\text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{P(A \cup B)}{P(A) \cdot P(B)} \leq \frac{P(A)}{P(A) \cdot P(B)} = \frac{1}{P(B)} = \frac{1}{\max(P(A), P(B))}$$ {#eq-lift-max}

The maximum theoretical lift for the pair $(A, B)$ is:

$$\text{lift}_{\max}(A, B) = \frac{1}{\max(P(A), P(B))}$$ {#eq-lift-max-formula}

This result has an immediate consequence: two frequent items —say, with support 0.50 each— have a maximum theoretical lift of just 2.0. Two rare items —with support 0.05 each— have a maximum theoretical lift of 20. An observed lift of 1.8 for the first pair represents 90% of its achievable maximum; the same lift of 1.8 for the second pair represents only 9%. The exact same figure signals a near-optimal rule in one case and a weak rule in the other.

To correct for this scale asymmetry, one can compute *relative lift*, defined as the fraction of theoretical maximum achieved by the observed lift:

$$\text{lift}_{\text{rel}}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{\text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B)}{\text{lift}_{\max}(A, B)} = \text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) \cdot \max(P(A), P(B))$$ {#eq-lift-relativo}

Relative lift spans the range $(0, 1]$ and is directly comparable across rules with different frequency levels. A relative lift near 1 indicates that items co-occur with maximum possible frequency given their individual supports.

## Complementary Metrics to Lift {#sec-metricas-complementarias}

The catalog of proposed metrics for evaluating association rule interest is extensive: @tan2006introduction systematically enumerate and compare over twenty distinct measures. None is universally superior; each captures a distinct facet of what we intuitively call "interest." This section details three metrics whose mathematical properties and complementarity with lift make them especially valuable in practice.

### Leverage: Absolute Difference Above Chance

Leverage for a rule $A \Rightarrow B$ measures the *absolute* difference between observed co-occurrence frequency and expected frequency under statistical independence:

$$\text{lev}(A \Rightarrow B) = P(A \cup B) - P(A) \cdot P(B)$$ {#eq-leverage}

This definition shares the exact structure of lift's numerator, minus the normalization by $P(A) \cdot P(B)$. Consequently, leverage preserves probability units, giving it an operationally direct interpretation: a leverage of 0.05 in a 1,000-transaction dataset means roughly 50 transactions contain both $A$ and $B$ beyond expectation under independence. This is the additional transaction count attributable to the association.

The theoretical range of leverage is $[-\min(P(A), P(B)) \cdot (1 - \max(P(A), P(B))),\; \min(P(A), P(B)) \cdot (1 - \max(P(A), P(B)))]$, centered at zero. Positive leverage indicates positive association; negative indicates inhibition; zero indicates independence —just like lift, but on an absolute rather than relative scale.

The key distinction between leverage and lift lies in scale. Lift normalizes deviation from independence into a dimensionless number comparable across rules with different consequents. Leverage gives the absolute magnitude of that deviation, which is more interpretable when quantifying rule *impact*: in recommendation engines, leverage is proportional to additional transactions generated by acting on the rule. However, this reliance on absolute frequencies makes leverage systematically low for rare items, even when their relative relationship is very strong —the exact inverse problem of absolute lift.

### Conviction: Asymmetry and Quasi-Determinism

Conviction for a rule $A \Rightarrow B$ was introduced to create a directional, asymmetric metric like confidence that simultaneously reflects departure from statistical independence [@tan2006introduction]:

$$\text{conv}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{1 - P(B)}{1 - \text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B)} = \frac{P(A) \cdot (1 - P(B))}{P(A) - P(A \cup B)}$$ {#eq-conviction}

Conviction's interpretation is less intuitive than lift or leverage, but warrants careful examination. The numerator $1 - P(B)$ is the expected frequency of rule "errors" —transactions where $B$ does not occur— under independence: if $A$ and $B$ were independent, a fraction $1 - P(B)$ of transactions containing $A$ would lack $B$. The denominator $1 - \text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B)$ is the observed frequency of those errors. The ratio measures how many times *fewer* errors the rule makes compared to expectation under independence.

Several notable properties follow:

- When $A$ and $B$ are independent, $\text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B) = P(B)$, and conviction $= (1 - P(B)) / (1 - P(B)) = 1$. Conviction equals 1 if and only if there is independence —just as lift equals 1 under independence.
- As confidence approaches 1, the denominator approaches 0, and conviction approaches infinity. A rule with very high conviction is quasi-deterministic: observing $A$ predicts $B$ with near certainty.
- When confidence is lower than $P(B)$ (negative association), conviction is less than 1.
- Unlike lift, conviction is asymmetric: $\text{conv}(A \Rightarrow B) \neq \text{conv}(B \Rightarrow A)$ in general, making it well-suited for evaluating rule strength in its specific direction.

This asymmetry is particularly valuable when rule direction carries operational meaning: in recommendation systems, medical diagnostics, or any domain where acting on an antecedent predicts a consequent.

### Zhang's Metric: Bounded Range and Robustness

Proposed to address limitations of lift, Zhang's metric is defined as [@tan2006introduction]:

$$Z(A, B) = \frac{P(A \cup B) - P(A) \cdot P(B)}{\max\left\{P(A \cup B)\cdot[1 - P(A)],\; P(A)\cdot[P(B) - P(A \cup B)]\right\}}$$ {#eq-zhang}

The numerator matches leverage: observed co-occurrence minus independence. The denominator normalizes the metric to ensure $Z \in [-1, 1]$ for any item pair, regardless of individual frequencies. This property —lacking in lift— makes Zhang's metric directly comparable across rules with varying frequency levels.

The denominator selects the maximum of two terms: $P(A \cup B) \cdot [1 - P(A)]$, which dominates under high co-occurrence (strong positive association), and $P(A) \cdot [P(B) - P(A \cup B)]$, which dominates under low co-occurrence (negative association or inhibition). This design guarantees a symmetric scale: $Z = 1$ corresponds to maximum co-occurrence ($A$ and $B$ co-occur whenever the rarer item occurs), while $Z = -1$ corresponds to minimum co-occurrence (mutual exclusion).

Zhang's metric is symmetric —like lift— and exceptionally robust against highly frequent items, which cause the most severe bias in confidence and leverage. For these reasons, @tan2006introduction recommend Zhang's metric as one of the most reliable measures for ranking rules by general interest when no prior domain knowledge is available.

## Sensitivity to Algorithm Parameters {#sec-sensibilidad}

### Thresholds as Decisions Lacking Intrinsic Statistical Basis

When invoking Apriori with specific parameters —e.g., `min_support = 0.10` and minimum lift of 1.0— decisions are made that, unlike parameters in formal statistical models, carry no intrinsic statistical justification. No theory establishes that the "correct" minimum support is 0.10 rather than 0.08 or 0.12. Analysts select these thresholds based on practical considerations —compute time, manageable result set size, domain knowledge— but the algorithm accepts them blindly.

This circumstance carries serious methodological consequences: the resulting rule set is not an objective property of the dataset, but a joint function of the dataset and chosen parameters. Two analysts working on the same dataset with slightly different thresholds can arrive at substantially different rule sets, potentially reaching conflicting conclusions if unaware of this dependence. Sound methodology requires exploring result stability across reasonable parameter shifts —termed *sensitivity analysis*— and reporting those parameters alongside findings.

### Sensitivity to Minimum Support

## Global Structure of Rule Space: Graph Representation {#sec-grafo}

### Motivation for a Network Representation

Previous sections represented rules as tables and metric scatter plots. Both forms help evaluate individual rules or small subsets, but obscure the *relational structure* connecting items globally. An item can participate in many rules as antecedent or consequent; two items may connect directly via a rule or indirectly through an intermediate item. This topological structure is invisible in tabular form.

Graph representations make this structure explicit. Formally, we construct a graph $G = (V, E)$ where vertex set $V$ comprises unique items participating in at least one filtered rule, and edge set $E$ contains directed edge $(A, B)$ for every rule $A \Rightarrow B$ exceeding minimum lift and support thresholds. To maintain readability, we restrict focus to single-item antecedents and consequents (1-itemsets).

### Centrality, Hubs, and Bridge Nodes

In graph theory, node *centrality* measures relative importance in the network. Among multiple definitions, a natural metric in association rules is the sum of lifts for all rules involving the item as antecedent or consequent. This weights items participating in many *strong* associations, rather than merely many associations.

```{python}
#| label: fig-grafo-reglas
#| fig-cap: "Association rule graph for single items with lift > 1.1 and support ≥ 0.10. Each node is an item; each edge is a rule meeting thresholds. Node size and color depict centrality (sum of lifts for connected rules). Large, bright nodes represent items involved in multiple strong associations; small, dark nodes are peripheral."

reglas_grafo = reglas_base[
    (reglas_base["lift"] > 1.1) &
    (reglas_base["support"] >= 0.10) &
    (reglas_base["antecedents"].apply(len) == 1) &
    (reglas_base["consequents"].apply(len) == 1)
].copy()

nodos = list(set(
    reglas_grafo["antecedents_str"].tolist() +
    reglas_grafo["consequents_str"].tolist()
))
n_nodos = len(nodos)
nodo_idx = {nd: i for i, nd in enumerate(nodos)}

angulos = [2 * math.pi * i / n_nodos for i in range(n_nodos)]
xs = [math.cos(a) for a in angulos]
ys = [math.sin(a) for a in angulos]

# Centrality by sum of lifts
centralidad = {}
for nd in nodos:
    r_sub = reglas_grafo[
        (reglas_grafo["antecedents_str"] == nd) |
        (reglas_grafo["consequents_str"] == nd)
    ]
    centralidad[nd] = r_sub["lift"].sum()

edge_x, edge_y = [], []
for _, row in reglas_grafo.iterrows():
    x0 = xs[nodo_idx[row["antecedents_str"]]]
    y0 = ys[nodo_idx[row["antecedents_str"]]]
    x1 = xs[nodo_idx[row["consequents_str"]]]
    y1 = ys[nodo_idx[row["consequents_str"]]]
    edge_x += [x0, x1, None]
    edge_y += [y0, y1, None]

fig = go.Figure()

fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=edge_x, y=edge_y,
    mode="lines",
    line=dict(width=1.2, color="#cccccc"),
    hoverinfo="none",
    showlegend=False,
))

fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=xs, y=ys,
    mode="markers+text",
    text=nodos,
    textposition="top center",
    textfont=dict(size=10),
    marker=dict(
        size=[max(12, centralidad.get(nd, 1) * 5) for nd in nodos],
        color=[centralidad.get(nd, 0) for nd in nodos],
        colorscale="Plasma",
        showscale=True,
        colorbar=dict(title="Centrality<br>(Σ lifts)"),
        line=dict(color="white", width=1),
    ),
    hovertext=[
        f"<b>{nd}</b><br>Centrality: {centralidad.get(nd, 0):.2f}<br>"
        f"Connected rules: {len(reglas_grafo[(reglas_grafo['antecedents_str']==nd)|(reglas_grafo['consequents_str']==nd)])}"
        for nd in nodos
    ],
    hoverinfo="text",
    showlegend=False,
))

fig.update_layout(
    xaxis=dict(showgrid=False, zeroline=False, showticklabels=False),
    yaxis=dict(showgrid=False, zeroline=False, showticklabels=False),
    height=540,
    plot_bgcolor="white",
    margin=dict(t=30, b=30, l=30, r=30),
)
fig.show()
```

@fig-grafo-reglas visualizes rule space topology. Highly central nodes participate in multiple strong associations, not simply high edge counts. An item can become a *hub* for two distinct reasons: being genuinely central to market basket co-purchases —its co-occurrences far exceed expectations from individual frequencies— or being so ubiquitous that it co-occurs with nearly everything, albeit with low lift.

Distinguishing genuine centrality from frequency-driven spurious centrality parallels the confidence issue: just as high confidence can artifactually arise from consequent base frequency, high network centrality can stem from sheer item ubiquity. Weighting centrality by lift —rather than raw co-occurrence counts— serves to correct this bias.

Equally important is the *bridge node* or *broker*: an item with few network neighbors connecting item clusters that would otherwise remain disconnected. In basket analysis, a bridge node might represent a niche item acting as a trigger for a specific purchasing chain. Such nodes are invisible in frequency-only analyses but offer high marketing actionability.

```{python}
    (reglas_base["lift"] > 1.2)

if len(candidatas) > 0:
    print("Rules with whole milk as consequent and lift > 1.2:")
    print(candidatas[["regla", "confidence", "lift"]].round(3).to_string(index=False))
else:
    print("No rules targeting whole milk exceed lift > 1.2.")
    print("Conclusion: whole milk is not a useful consequent in this dataset.")
```

The analysis confirms theoretical predictions: when $P(B)$ is very high, the headroom for a rule $A \Rightarrow B$ to be genuinely nontrivial shrinks dramatically. Achieving lift > 1.5 requires $\text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B) > 1.5 \times 0.58 \approx 0.87$: nearly 9 out of 10 transactions containing $A$ must also include whole milk. This condition is nearly impossible in general retail where milk is ubiquitous. The takeaway is not dataset defectiveness, but that *whole milk* makes a poor consequent in association rule mining: its ubiquity prevents rules predicting it from yielding actionable insight.

Generalizing, for any item $B$, the confidence threshold required for a rule to be nontrivial by factor $k$ is:

$$\text{conf}(A \Rightarrow B) > k \cdot P(B) \qquad \text{equivalently,} \qquad \text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) > k$$ {#eq-umbral-conf}

This relationship enables systematically building consequent-specific threshold tables for analysts to consult prior to interpreting rules.

```{python}
#| label: tbl-umbrales-por-item
#| tbl-cap: "Minimum confidence thresholds required for a rule to be nontrivial (lift > 1.0) or moderately interesting (lift > 1.5), computed for each item as a potential consequent. Items with high P(item) require very high confidence to yield genuinely informative rules."
```

## An Integrated Evaluation Framework {#sec-marco-integrado}

### From Fragmentation to Coherence

The concepts developed across this chapter —lift limitations, complementary metrics, parameter sensitivity, graph structure— are not isolated components. They form a unified framework answering a core question: *how much confidence does a specific rule warrant?*

Answering this question involves multiple dimensions:

1. **Relative Dimension**: How far does observed lift deviate from independence relative to theoretical maximum given item supports? Relative lift addresses this.

2. **Absolute Dimension**: How many additional transactions beyond baseline independence exhibit co-occurrence? Leverage answers this.

3. **Directional Predictive Dimension**: How deterministic is the rule in its specific direction? Conviction addresses this.

4. **Normalized Robustness Dimension**: Is the association strong on a scale comparable across varying item frequencies? Zhang's metric resolves this.

5. **Parametric Stability Dimension**: Does the rule persist across reasonable threshold choices, or vanish under slight $\sigma_{\min}$ tightening? Sensitivity analysis addresses this.

A rule showing strength across all dimensions earns high confidence. A rule succeeding in only one or two calls for caution. The following function systematically executes this multidimensional evaluation:

```{python}
#| label: pipeline-evaluacion-integrada

def evaluar_regla_completa(row, n_transacciones=50, verbose=True):
    """
    Multidimensional evaluation of an association rule.
    Returns qualitative classification: ROBUST / CANDIDATE / DISCARD
    """
    criterios = []
    puntos = 0

    # Dimension 1: Lift
    if row["lift"] > 1.5:
        criterios.append("Strong lift (> 1.5)")
        puntos += 3
    elif row["lift"] > 1.1:
        criterios.append("Moderate lift (1.1 – 1.5)")
        puntos += 1
    else:
        criterios.append("Weak lift (≤ 1.1) — indistinguishable from chance")

    # Dimension 2: Absolute support
    n_obs = int(row["support"] * n_transacciones)
    if n_obs >= 10:
        criterios.append(f"Solid support ({n_obs} of {n_transacciones} transactions)")
        puntos += 2
    elif n_obs >= 5:
        criterios.append(f"Minimum support ({n_obs} of {n_transacciones} transactions)")
        puntos += 1
    else:
        criterios.append(f"Insufficient support ({n_obs} of {n_transacciones} transactions)")

    # Dimension 3: Zhang (normalized robustness)
    if row["zhang"] > 0.3:
        criterios.append("Robust Zhang (> 0.3)")
        puntos += 2
    elif row["zhang"] > 0.1:
        criterios.append("Moderate Zhang (0.1 – 0.3)")
        puntos += 1
    else:
        criterios.append("Weak Zhang (≤ 0.1) — likely base frequency artifact")

    # Dimension 4: Conviction (directional determinism)
    if row["conviction"] > 1.5:
        criterios.append("High conviction (> 1.5) — quasi-deterministic rule")
        puntos += 1

    clasificacion = (
        "ROBUST"    if puntos >= 6 else
        "CANDIDATE"  if puntos >= 3 else
        "DISCARD"
    )

    if verbose:
        print(f"{'─'*55}")
        print(f"Rule:  {row['regla']}")
        print(f"{'─'*55}")
        for c in criterios:
            print(f"  • {c}")
        print(f"\n  → Classification: {clasificacion}  ({puntos}/8 points)\n")

    return clasificacion

# Evaluate top 6 rules by lift
print("Evaluation of top 6 rules by lift:\n")
for _, row in reglas_base.sort_values("lift", ascending=False).head(6).iterrows():
    evaluar_regla_completa(row)
```

## Conceptual Summary {#sec-resumen-cap2}

This chapter explored association rule mining beyond fundamental metrics. Key takeaways:

**On Structural Limitations of Lift:**

- Lift is symmetric: $\text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) = \text{lift}(B \Rightarrow A)$. Selecting rule direction for practical applications relies on confidence —an asymmetric metric.
- Lift has a theoretical ceiling dependent on individual supports: $\text{lift}_{\max} = 1 / \max(P(A), P(B))$. Comparing raw lifts across rules with differing supports can be misleading. Relative lift —ratio of observed to maximum lift— provides a fairer basis for comparison.

**On Complementary Metrics:**

- Leverage measures absolute difference between observed co-occurrence and independence. It reflects additional transactions attributable to association, but is systematically low for rare items.
- Conviction is asymmetric and measures rule quasi-determinism in direction: very high values signal confidence near 1.
- Zhang's metric spans $[-1, 1]$, is symmetric, and resists frequent-item bias. It is recommended for comparing rules across varying frequency regimes.
- Metrics are non-redundant: imperfect correlations mean metric discrepancies offer key analytical signals.

**On Parameter Sensitivity:**

- Apriori thresholds carry no intrinsic statistical justification. Resulting rule sets are a joint function of dataset and chosen parameters.
- Sensitivity analysis —systematic exploration of parameter space— is a mandatory methodological practice.
- The support-confidence map visualizes full decision space, letting analysts deliberately position choices along the quantity vs. quality curve.

**On Graph Representations:**

- Rule graphs reveal network topology: hubs, bridge nodes, and item clusters.
- Lift-weighted centrality —rather than raw edge counts— distinguishes genuinely central items from frequency-driven artifacts.
- The "Whole Milk Problem" demonstrates how high consequent frequency makes discovering informative rules nearly impossible regardless of thresholds.

**On the Integrated Framework:**

- Robust rule evaluation requires examining at least four dimensions simultaneously: relative lift, leverage, Zhang, and conviction.
- Robust rules show convergence across multiple metrics; candidate rules show partial convergence; discarded rules fail under stricter metrics.

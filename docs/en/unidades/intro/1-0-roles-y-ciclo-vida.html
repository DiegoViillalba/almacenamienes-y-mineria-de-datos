2  Data Team Roles and the Data Lifecycle

In modern organizations, analytics projects are rarely developed by a single individual. As data volume grows and technologies specialize, the need for multidisciplinary teams arises. According to the DAMA-DMBOK data governance framework (DAMA International 2017) and the characterization of the data scientist role by Davenport and Patil (2012), there are four main profiles, each with specific responsibilities, tools, and deliverables.

2.0.1 Comparison of Data Engineering and Data Science Roles

2.1 The Data Lifecycle

Definition (Data Lifecycle). The sequence of phases through which information passes within an organization, from initial planning to eventual secure disposal, passing through ingestion, storage, and analysis (DAMA International 2017).

The data lifecycle describes the sequence of phases through which information passes within an organization. This cycle is fundamental for structuring the development of this course: Part I (Data Warehouses) mainly covers the capture and storage phases, while Part II (Data Mining) focuses on strategic and predictive analysis.

Neither infrastructure management (storage phase) nor applied analytics (exploration and modeling phase) occurs in isolation. They are integrated within a sequential and iterative process for data projects. Before building any model or database, we must go through stages that help us evaluate project viability: business understanding, data collection and inspection, preprocessing, and subsequent evaluation of results to ensure successful production deployment. This structured approach is what sets professional analytical projects apart from academic proofs-of-concept.

The lifecycle consists of six key stages (Google 2022):

  1. Plan: Determine data requirements to solve the business problem, define necessary sources, and identify the required team.
  2. Capture: Collect data from internal and external sources (transactional databases, web logs, APIs). In this phase, data is extracted and prepared.
  3. Manage: Store data in a structured manner within relational databases or analytical data warehouses. Here, security policies, initial cleaning, and data lineage are enforced.
  4. Analyze: Explore data using statistics and data mining (EDA), and train analytical models to answer key questions or automate processes.
  5. Archive: Move historical data that is no longer queried frequently to low-cost passive storage systems (e.g., Glacier or Google Cloud Coldline Storage), safeguarding it against accidental modifications.
  6. Destroy: Permanently delete data once the required retention period has expired, ensuring that no residual or unsecure copies of personally identifiable information (PII) remain under secure erasure policies.

2.2 Critical Phases of Data Management

Regardless of the stage of the lifecycle, two questions cross the entire process: how sensitive is the data being handled and who is permitted to access it. Neither is resolved informally; both are governed through explicit policies that the data team must know before touching a single table.

2.2.1 Data Requiring Special Handling

Not all data presents the same risk level if exposed or misused. Certain categories—known as sensitive data or special categories—require additional collection, storage, and access controls:

  • Personally Identifiable Information (PII): Name, address, official ID number, email, or any data allowing a person to be directly or indirectly identified.
  • Health Data: Clinical records, diagnoses, or lab results; explicitly protected in Mexico as sensitive personal data under the Federal Law on Protection of Personal Data Held by Private Parties (Congreso de los Estados Unidos Mexicanos 2010).
  • Financial and Biometric Data: Credit card numbers, credit history, fingerprints, or facial recognition.

Thus, before capturing any of these data types, the minimization principle is applied: collect only what is strictly necessary to answer the business question, rather than hoarding sensitive information “just in case”, which increases risk without contributing analytical value.

2.2.2 Governance, Usage Policy, and Access Control

The most common way to mitigate these risks is working with a data usage policy: a formal document defining what data exists, who can query it, under what conditions, and for what purpose. In modern enterprises, this policy is typically designed by the Data Architect alongside legal and security teams, and translates operationally into:

  • Least Privilege Access Control: Each user or role receives specific access capabilities limited strictly to data essential for their function—a Data Analyst can query aggregated sales by region, but not necessarily individual credit card numbers—implemented via Identity and Access Management (IAM) policies and Role-Based Access Control (RBAC).
  • Encryption: Of data at rest and in transit.
  • Data Catalog: An updated inventory documenting sensitive fields, data owners (data owners), and applicable retention rules, preventing misinterpretations between teams.

2.3 Guided Activity: Designing a Lifecycle Plan for TheLook eCommerce

As an introductory practical activity for this course, you will design the initial data plan for the fictitious company TheLook eCommerce, based on requirement requests from the executive board of the Marketing and Sales departments.

2.3.1 Business Scenario

TheLook eCommerce has grown rapidly thanks to its omnichannel offering. However, its current data infrastructure is chaotic. The Marketing team has identified four main business questions they need answered to launch effective campaigns next quarter.

2.3.2 Goal Alignment and Data Planning Exercise

For each business question, we will associate its analytical objective and identify the necessary data that must be captured and managed in our future Data Warehouse.

# Business Question (Stakeholder) Analytical Objective Data Required to Capture
1 “Which products should we promote together on our homepage?” Discover association rules between product categories co-purchased by users. • Order history (orders and order_items).
• Product ID and clickstream history from browsing sessions.
2 “How can we optimize inventory levels of women’s dresses across regional warehouses?” Predict weekly future demand for specific products by geographical location. • Daily warehouse inventory logs (inventory_items).
• Shipping addresses and postal codes of historical purchases.
3 “What is the long-term cumulative value of customers acquired through paid Google ads?” Calculate Customer Lifetime Value (CLV) grouped by acquisition channel. • User tables (users) with registration date and referral channel.

• Payment methods and elapsed time between delivery and return request.

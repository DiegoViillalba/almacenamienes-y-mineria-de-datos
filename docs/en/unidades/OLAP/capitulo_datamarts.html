17  Data Marts: From the Data Warehouse to Business Unit Consumption

Architecture, Dimensional Design, and Reproducible Example

Author

Diego Villalba

Published

August 5, 2026

In the previous chapter, an OLAP data cube was defined as an aggregation function over dimensions: \(C:D_1\times\cdots\times D_k\to\mathbb{R}^m\). To calculate this function efficiently, data must be organized dimensionally. A fundamental question follows: where do the underlying fact and dimension tables queried by the cube originate? In most enterprise environments, the answer is a data mart: a specialized subset of the enterprise data warehouse tailored to a specific business domain, serving as the foundation for cubes, dashboards, reports, and analytical consumption APIs.

This chapter formalizes the concept of data marts, evaluates architectural paradigms (dependent, independent, and hybrid), introduces Kimball’s dimensional design framework, and applies it to an end-to-end retail hardware case study (Kimball and Ross 2013; Inmon 2005). The data mart built here serves as the authoritative backend for the Location × Time × Department cube explored previously.

17.1 What is a Data Mart?

An enterprise data warehouse (DW) integrates data across multiple business processes—sales, inventory, human resources, finance—into a single, historical, consistent repository. However, individual business analysts rarely require access to the entire warehouse. The Sales team focuses on transaction records; Human Resources analyzes payroll and turnover. Querying a monolithic DW containing dozens of fact tables and hundreds of attributes creates performance bottlenecks and unnecessary analytical complexity.

Definition. A data mart is a specialized, domain-specific subset of a data warehouse focused on a single functional area or business process, designed to serve the analytical queries of a dedicated user group. \[ DM_j \subseteq DW, \qquad DM_j = \pi_{A_j}\big(\sigma_{P_j}(DW)\big) \]

Here, \(\sigma_{P_j}\) selects fact records corresponding to business process \(P_j\) (e.g., retail sales), and \(\pi_{A_j}\) projects only the relevant dimensions and attributes for business unit \(A_j\). The result is a streamlined analytical model that is faster to query and simpler to analyze (Devlin and Murphy 1988).

Three characteristics distinguish a data mart from an enterprise data warehouse:

  1. Focused Scope: Serves a single business process or department rather than the entire enterprise.
  2. Targeted Audience: Optimized for specific business questions and operational workflows.
  3. Explicit Dimensional Schema: Implemented as star or snowflake schemas optimized for analytical aggregations.

17.2 Architectural Approaches: Dependent, Independent, and Hybrid

How a data mart ingests data—either downstream from a central DW or directly from operational source systems—defines its architectural model and determines data consistency across the enterprise.

17.2.1 Dependent Data Marts (Inmon Top-Down Approach)

In Bill Inmon’s corporate information factory (CIF) framework, an Enterprise Data Warehouse (EDW) is constructed first as a fully normalized repository (Third Normal Form, 3NF) integrating all source data (Inmon 2005). Dependent data marts are constructed downstream as dimensional views derived from the centralized EDW.

Definition. A data mart is dependent when its sole data source is the integrated enterprise data warehouse. \[ DM_j = f_j(DW), \qquad \text{with } DW \text{ as single source of truth} \]

The primary advantage is strict enterprise consistency: because all data marts derive from a single integrated repository, dimensions like Customer, Product, or Employee retain identical definitions across all data marts. The primary drawback is implementation speed: building a normalized enterprise DW before releasing the first data mart requires substantial upfront time and capital.

17.2.2 Independent Data Marts (Bottom-Up Approach)

In the bottom-up approach associated with Ralph Kimball, organizations bypass building a centralized 3NF warehouse first. Instead, independent data marts are built directly from operational source systems to serve immediate business needs.

Definition. A data mart is independent when built directly from operational source systems without an intermediary enterprise warehouse. \[ DM_j = g_j(S_1, S_2, \ldots, S_n), \qquad S_i \text{ = operational source systems} \]

The advantage is rapid time-to-value: individual departments can build and deploy data marts within weeks. The critical risk is the creation of uncoordinated data silos: if Sales and Marketing define “active customer” or “net revenue” differently, cross-departmental metrics will conflict.

17.2.3 Hybrid Architecture: The Kimball Bus Architecture

Kimball reconciles time-to-value with enterprise consistency through the Data Warehouse Bus Architecture. Data marts are built iteratively, process by process, but share a set of conformed dimensions—standardized, enterprise-wide definitions for Time, Product, Customer, Location, etc., agreed upon before development (Kimball and Ross 2013). The collection of conformed data marts forms a virtual Enterprise Data Warehouse without requiring a monolithic 3NF repository upfront.

17.3 Conformed Dimensions and the Bus Matrix

Definition. A conformed dimension is a dimension table maintaining identical keys, attributes, and business definitions across multiple data marts, enabling cross-process drill-across analysis without transformation.

The primary planning framework for identifying conformed dimensions is the Data Warehouse Bus Matrix: a matrix mapping business processes (rows) to candidate dimensions (columns).

17.4 Four-Step Dimensional Design Process

Kimball outlines four sequential steps for designing any dimensional data mart (Kimball and Ross 2013):

  1. Select the Business Process: Identify the operational activity to model (e.g., retail point-of-sale transactions).
  2. Declare the Grain: Define what an individual fact table row represents atomically.
  3. Identify the Dimensions: Determine the surrounding context (Who, What, Where, When, Why).
  4. Identify the Facts: Select the numeric measures accumulated by the business process.

17.4.1 Case Study: Hardware Retail Sales Data Mart

Applying this process to our hardware retail case study:

  1. Business Process: Store sales transactions.
  2. Grain: One row per store transaction line item per department per quarter.
  3. Dimensions: dim_tiempo (quarter, year), dim_ubicacion (city, region), dim_departamento (department, category).
  4. Facts: monto_venta (additive revenue measure), unidades (additive quantity measure).

17.5 Reproducible Python Data Mart Implementation

We construct this physical data mart using pandas:

import pandas as pd

dim_tiempo = pd.DataFrame({
    "time_key": [1, 2],
    "quarter": ["Q1", "Q2"],
    "year": [2025, 2025],
})

dim_ubicacion = pd.DataFrame({
    "location_key": [1, 2, 3, 4],
    "city": ["CDMX", "Guadalajara", "Monterrey", "Merida"],
    "region": ["Central", "West", "North", "Southeast"],
})

dim_departamento = pd.DataFrame({
    "dept_key": [1, 2, 3, 4],
    "department": ["Wood", "Plumbing", "Decoration", "Electrical"],
})

# fact_ventas: (time_key, location_key, dept_key, sales_amount)
fact_ventas = pd.DataFrame([
    (1, 1, 1, 70), (1, 1, 2, 40), (1, 1, 3, 20), (1, 1, 4, 30),
    (2, 1, 1, 50), (2, 1, 2, 60), (2, 1, 3, 22), (2, 1, 4, 28),
    (1, 2, 1, 60), (1, 2, 2, 35), (1, 2, 3, 25), (1, 2, 4, 40),
    (2, 2, 1, 45), (2, 2, 2, 50), (2, 2, 3, 30), (2, 2, 4, 35),
    (1, 3, 1, 48), (1, 3, 2, 38), (1, 3, 3, 18), (1, 3, 4, 26),
    (2, 3, 1, 36), (2, 3, 2, 55), (2, 3, 3, 25), (2, 3, 4, 30),
    (1, 4, 1, 52), (1, 4, 2, 42), (1, 4, 3, 15), (1, 4, 4, 37),
    (2, 4, 1, 44), (2, 4, 2, 33), (2, 4, 3, 20), (2, 4, 4, 28),
], columns=["time_key", "location_key", "dept_key", "sales_amount"])

The equivalent SQL query joining facts to dimensions:

SELECT
    u.region,
    t.quarter,
    d.department,
    SUM(f.sales_amount) AS total_sales
FROM fact_ventas f
JOIN dim_tiempo       t ON f.time_key = t.time_key
JOIN dim_ubicacion    u ON f.location_key = u.location_key
JOIN dim_departamento d ON f.dept_key = d.dept_key
GROUP BY u.region, t.quarter, d.department
ORDER BY u.region, t.quarter, d.department

In pandas, this dimensional join and aggregation is expressed as:

report = (
    fact_ventas
    .merge(dim_tiempo, on="time_key")
    .merge(dim_ubicacion, on="location_key")
    .merge(dim_departamento, on="dept_key")
    .groupby(["region", "quarter", "department"], as_index=False)["sales_amount"]
    .sum()
    .rename(columns={"sales_amount": "total_sales"})
    .sort_values(["region", "quarter", "department"])
)

17.5.1 Performing a Regional Roll-Up Query

Rolling up locations to macro zones:

zone_map = pd.DataFrame({
    "region": ["Central", "West", "North", "Southeast"],
    "zone": ["North-Central Zone", "West-Southeast Zone",
             "North-Central Zone", "West-Southeast Zone"],
})

zone_report = (
    fact_ventas
    .merge(dim_tiempo, on="time_key")
    .merge(dim_ubicacion, on="location_key")
    .merge(zone_map, on="region")
    .groupby(["zone", "quarter"], as_index=False)["sales_amount"]
    .sum()
    .rename(columns={"sales_amount": "total_sales"})
    .sort_values(["zone", "quarter"])
)

17.6 Data Marts to Data Products

In modern data architectures, data marts have evolved into data products within a data mesh framework: domain teams own their data marts as products complete with SLAs, quality contracts, and standardized consumption APIs (Dehghani 2022). The next chapter explores how to expose this data mart via REST and semantic layer APIs.

17.7 Conclusions

A data mart bridges raw operational data and business analytics. Adopting a Kimball bus architecture with conformed dimensions guarantees that individual data marts scale incrementally without degrading into isolated data silos.

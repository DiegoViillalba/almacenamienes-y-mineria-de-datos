34  Dimensionality Reduction with PCA

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) has evolved significantly in recent years. While classic approaches focused on descriptive statistics and basic visualizations, modern multivariate EDA recognizes that real-world data is inherently high-dimensional and that understanding it requires integrating multiple analytical levels in a structured manner.

35 The Curse of Dimensionality

When the number of variables \(p\) grows, spatial geometry becomes deeply counterintuitive. The phenomenon known as the curse of dimensionality (Bellman, 1957) describes how spatial volume expands so rapidly that data points inevitably become sparse.

Consider a hypersphere of radius \(r\) inscribed within a hypercube of side length \(2r\) in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). The ratio of sphere volume to hypercube volume is:

\[ \frac{V_{\text{sphere}}}{V_{\text{cube}}} = \frac{\pi^{d/2} r^d}{\Gamma(d/2 + 1) \cdot (2r)^d} = \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{2^d \, \Gamma(d/2 + 1)} \]

In 2 and 3 dimensions, this ratio is relatively large (~78% and ~52% respectively), as shown in the following interactive visualization where \(r=1\).

However, this ratio tends to zero as \(d \to \infty\). This means that most of the hypercube’s volume concentrates in the corners, while the sphere (representing the region of interest) occupies a diminishing fraction of the total space.

This implies that for high-dimensional data analysis, maintaining a fixed sampling density \(\rho\) in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) requires the number of observations to scale as:

\[ n \sim \rho \cdot L^d \]

With \(p = 20\) features, there are \(\binom{20}{2} = 190\) pairwise correlations. With \(p = 100\), there are 4,950. Manually inspecting every relationship becomes impossible. Thus, we need methods that compress information without sacrificing core structure.

36 Mathematical Foundations of PCA

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a dimensionality reduction technique that transforms a dataset with correlated features into a new system of uncorrelated variables called principal components. Each component is a linear combination of original features and represents a direction of maximum variance, preserving as much original data information as possible.

36.1 Mathematical Formulation and Procedure

Given a dataset \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\), where \(n\) is the number of samples and \(p\) the number of features, the process follows a precise sequence:

  1. Standardization and Centering

    To prevent measurement scales from distorting the analysis, variables are transformed to a shared scale:

    • Centering: subtract the mean of each feature
    • Scaling: divide by its standard deviation

    This ensures each variable has zero mean and unit variance.

    \[ \tilde{X}_{ij} = \frac{X_{ij} - \mu_j}{\sigma_j} \]

    where:

    • \(\mu_j\) is the mean of feature \(j\)
    • \(\sigma_j\) is its standard deviation

  2. Covariance Matrix Calculation: The sample covariance matrix \(S\) summarizes feature variability and pairwise relationships:

    \[S = \frac{1}{n-1} \tilde{X}^T \tilde{X}\]

    Element-wise:

    \[s_{jk} = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_{ij} - \mu_j)(x_{ik} - \mu_k)\]

    where:

    • \(s_{jj}\) represents the variance of feature \(j\)
    • \(s_{jk}\) represents the covariance between features \(j\) and \(k\)

    This matrix is symmetric and positive semi-definite, guaranteeing real, non-negative eigenvalues.

  3. Spectral Decomposition: The eigenvalue problem is solved to identify principal directions: \[S \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}\] Here, \(\lambda\) represents the eigenvalues (explained variance in that direction) and \(\mathbf{v}\) the eigenvectors (loadings/directions).

  4. Optimization and Ranking: The first principal component \(w_1\) is the eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\). Subsequent components maximize remaining variance subject to orthogonality constraints: \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_p\).

  5. Projection and Reduction: Finally, original data points are projected onto the subspace spanned by the top \(k\) components: \[\mathbf{Z} = \tilde{X} \mathbf{V}_k\] where \(\mathbf{V}_k\) contains the first \(k\) ranked eigenvectors.

Each component captures a fraction of total data variance (\(\sum \lambda_j\)). The contribution of each component is measured by the Proportion of Variance Explained (PVE):

\[\text{PVE}_k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j}\]

This enables reducing dimensionality while retaining, for instance, 95% of cumulative original variance.

36.2 Practical Example: Step-by-Step Manual Calculation

We demonstrate the full process using a two-variable raw dataset (\(p=2\)):

  1. Original Data Matrix: Suppose \(n=3\) observations across \(p=2\) features: \[X = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\]

  2. Data Centering: Column means are \(\bar{x}_1 = 3\) and \(\bar{x}_2 = 4\). Subtracting means yields \(\tilde{X}\): \[\tilde{X} = \begin{pmatrix} 2-3 & 3-4 \\ 3-3 & 4-4 \\ 4-3 & 5-4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\]

  3. Covariance Matrix (\(S\)): Applying \(S = \frac{1}{n-1} \tilde{X}^T \tilde{X}\) with \(n-1 = 2\): \[S = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\]

  4. Eigenvalue Computation (\(\lambda\)): Solving \(\det(S - \lambda I) = 0\): \[\det \begin{pmatrix} 1-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 1-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (1-\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0 \implies \lambda^2 - 2\lambda = 0\] Yielding \(\lambda_1 = 2\) and \(\lambda_2 = 0\). Total variance is \(2+0=2\). The first component explains 100% of variance.

  5. Eigenvector Computation (\(\mathbf{v}\)): For \(\lambda_1 = 2\), solving \((S - 2I)\mathbf{v}_1 = 0\): \[\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_{11} \\ v_{21} \end{pmatrix} = 0 \implies v_{11} = v_{21}\] Normalizing so \(\|v\|=1\): \(v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.707 \\ 0.707 \end{pmatrix}\).

  6. Data Projection (\(k=1\)): Projecting centered data onto PC1: \(Z = \tilde{X} v_1\): \[Z = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0.707 \\ 0.707 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1.414 \\ 0 \\ 1.414 \end{pmatrix}\]

  7. Conclusion: Since original data points were perfectly colinear (\(x_2 = x_1 + 1\)), PCA reduces dimensionality from 2D to 1D with zero information loss.

37 PCA and Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Modern software libraries computing PCA (e.g., scikit-learn) do not perform direct eigendecomposition of \(S\). Instead, they apply Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) directly to the centered matrix \(\tilde{X}\), providing superior numerical stability and efficiency.

Any matrix \(\tilde{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) can be decomposed as:

\[ \tilde{X} = U \Sigma V^T \]

where:

  • \(U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\): orthogonal matrix of left singular vectors
  • \(\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\): diagonal matrix of singular values \(\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_{\min(n,p)} \geq 0\)
  • \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}\): orthogonal matrix of right singular vectors

The connection between SVD and PCA is direct:

\[ S = \frac{1}{n-1}\tilde{X}^T\tilde{X} = \frac{1}{n-1} V \Sigma^2 V^T \]

Therefore:

  • Columns of \(V\) match the eigenvectors of \(S\)
  • Eigenvalues of \(S\) equal \(\lambda_k = \sigma_k^2 / (n-1)\)
  • Principal component scores are \(Z = \tilde{X}V = U\Sigma\)
import numpy as np
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris

X = load_iris().data
X_std = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

S = np.cov(X_std.T)
eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(S)
idx = np.argsort(eigenvalues)[::-1]
eigenvalues = eigenvalues[idx]
eigenvectors = eigenvectors[:, idx]

U, sigma, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X_std, full_matrices=False)
lambda_svd = sigma**2 / (len(X_std) - 1)

print("Eigenvalues via S:  ", np.round(eigenvalues, 6))
print("Eigenvalues via SVD:", np.round(lambda_svd, 6))
print("Identical results:", np.allclose(eigenvalues, lambda_svd))
Eigenvalues via S:   [2.938085 0.920165 0.147742 0.020854]
Eigenvalues via SVD: [2.938085 0.920165 0.147742 0.020854]

39 Limitations of PCA

39.1 Linearity

PCA evaluates linear variance directions and cannot unroll non-linear manifolds.

39.2 Outlier Sensitivity

Minimizing mean squared reconstruction error renders PCA highly sensitive to outliers.

39.3 Class Unawareness

PCA is unsupervised and maximizes overall variance without considering class labels. Supervised alternatives like Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) explicitly maximize class separability.

40 Beyond PCA: Non-Linear Techniques

Non-linear dimension reduction techniques like t-SNE and UMAP excel at preserving local manifold relationships.

  2. Impact of Standardization: Apply PCA to Iris with and without feature scaling. Justify mathematically why loadings and variance ratios alter.
  3. Reconstruction Error: Given dataset \(X\) and rank-\(k\) PCA projection, reconstruct \(\hat{X} = Z_k W_k^T + \mu\). Plot Frobenius reconstruction error \(\|X - \hat{X}\|_F\) as a function of \(k\).

  5. Kaiser Rule Proof: Prove why Kaiser’s \(\lambda > 1\) rule strictly applies to standardized correlation matrices.
